1. Number Systems & Binary Math

A numeral system expresses numbers using a consistent base (radix). Modern computing relies heavily on base 2 (Binary) and base 16 (Hexadecimal) due to electronic transistor states.

Positional Notation: $$\text{Value} = \sum_{i=0}^{n} d_i \times \text{base}^i$$
  • Binary (Base 2): Uses bits 0 and 1. Weights: $2^0=1, 2^1=2, 2^2=4, 2^3=8$.
  • Hexadecimal (Base 16): Uses 0-9 and A-F (where A=10, B=11, C=12, D=13, E=14, F=15). Each hex digit matches exactly 4 binary bits (a nibble).
Worked Example: Convert Decimal 25 to Binary:
• $25 \div 2 = 12 \text{ R } 1$
• $12 \div 2 = 6 \text{ R } 0$
• $6 \div 2 = 3 \text{ R } 0$
• $3 \div 2 = 1 \text{ R } 1$
• $1 \div 2 = 0 \text{ R } 1$
Reading remainders from bottom to top: (25)₁₀ = (11001)₂.

2. Matrices & Linear Transformations

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers arranged in rows and columns. Matrices model linear transformations, solve simultaneous equation systems, and power 3D graphics.

Matrix Multiplication Rule:
To multiply $A \times B$, the number of columns in $A$ must equal the number of rows in $B$.
If $A$ is of size $m \times k$ and $B$ is of size $k \times n$, then the product $AB$ has size $m \times n$.
  • Determinant: A scalar value encoding the volume scaling factor of the transformation. A matrix is singular (non-invertible) if and only if $\det(A) = 0$.
  • 2×2 Determinant Formula: $\det \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} = ad - bc$.
  • 2×2 Inverse Formula: $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)} \begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}$.
Worked Example: Determinant of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$:
$\det(A) = (4)(3) - (2)(1) = 12 - 2 = 10 \neq 0$.
Since $\det(A) \neq 0$, $A$ is invertible!

3. Algebra & Quadratic Equations

Algebra is built upon the fundamental principle of equality: performing identical operations on both sides preserves truth. For polynomial equations, quadratic forms represent parabolas.

The Quadratic Formula: $$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$
  • The Discriminant $\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$:
  • If $\Delta > 0$: Two distinct real roots (parabola crosses x-axis twice).
  • If $\Delta = 0$: One repeated real root (parabola touches x-axis at vertex).
  • If $\Delta < 0$: Two complex conjugate roots $u \pm vi$ where $i = \sqrt{-1}$.
Worked Example: Solve $x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$:
• $a = 1, b = -5, c = 6$
• $\Delta = (-5)^2 - 4(1)(6) = 25 - 24 = 1$
• $x = \frac{-(-5) \pm \sqrt{1}}{2(1)} = \frac{5 \pm 1}{2}$
• $x_1 = 3, x_2 = 2$.

4. Trigonometry & The Unit Circle

Trigonometry connects geometric angles with harmonic oscillations. On a unit circle of radius $r=1$, any angle $\theta$ defines coordinates $(x, y) = (\cos \theta, \sin \theta)$.

Radians to Degrees Conversion:
$\pi \text{ radians} = 180^\circ \implies 1 \text{ rad} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57.296^\circ$.
  • Sine ($\sin$): The vertical coordinate on the unit circle. Has range $[-1, 1]$ and period $2\pi$.
  • Cosine ($\cos$): The horizontal coordinate on the unit circle. Shifts $\sin$ by $\pi/2$.
  • Pythagorean Identity: $\sin^2(\theta) + \cos^2(\theta) = 1$ for all real $\theta$.
Worked Example: Evaluate $\sin(30^\circ)$:
In degrees: $\sin(30^\circ) = 0.5$.
In radians: $30^\circ = \pi/6 \text{ rad} \implies \sin(\pi/6) = 0.5$.
